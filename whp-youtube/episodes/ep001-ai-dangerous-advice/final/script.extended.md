## 2. Knowing the trick is not the same as checking

[DEFENSE — Voices the viewer's remaining immunity claim so evidence can test it.]

> Maybe your answer is still no — you know these tools make things up, you stay

[TRANSITION — Compressed label experiment: identical diagnoses, planted mistakes, changed byline.]

> In 2021, researchers gave over a hundred radiologists the same written diagnoses to
> check. [F-009](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-021-00385-9) Some of the diagnoses had planted mistakes. [F-009](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-021-00385-9)
>
> Half were told a doctor wrote them. Half were told an AI did. [F-009](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-021-00385-9)
>
> The AI group trusted the diagnoses less. [F-009](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-021-00385-9)

[TRANSITION — States the expected outcome and the narrator's confirmed bet so the reversal lands in contrast.]

[MINI-HOOK — No-real-model boundary hands the investigation to the actual chat.]

## 3. Move one — the question picks the menu

[MINI-HOOK — Names move one.]

> *Move one. The question picks the menu.*

[TRANSITION — Case return with new information: the documented question he typed, against the decision question.]

> Here's what he actually typed: "Is it possible to have visual disturbance after
> catheter ablation?" — the heart procedure he'd had. [F-010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006786/)
>
> It's a real question. Polite, specific, medically sensible. And notice: it doesn't
> even say "I". It asks whether something is possible — in general.
>
> And it is not the question his life depended on. That one was: is it safe to stay
> home with these symptoms?

[TRANSITION — The comforting answer available to the narrower question.]

> And "is it possible?" has a comforting answer available — yes, possibly, often
> temporary. [F-010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006786/)

[AHA | LOCKED WORDING — The possible-answer/safe-decision distinction, locked.]

> **A possible explanation is not a safety decision.**

[TRANSITION — Framing effect named and grounded in the childhood menu.]

> Psychology calls the force underneath this the framing effect: how you present a
> problem can change how people judge it. [F-003](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683)
>
> You learned it as a child, from the other side. A parent doesn't ask, "Do you want
> vegetables?" A parent asks, "Do you want broccoli, or carrots?"
>
> The child feels free. The menu was decided before they said a word.

[AHA | LOCKED WORDING — Role-reversal punchline: the viewer is the parent.]

> When you type a question into a chatbot, you're the parent now. **The AI is the
> child, politely picking a vegetable.**

[TRANSITION — Reinterprets the documented hope as the menu's author.]

> And nobody loads a question against themselves on purpose. He wasn't lying to
> ChatGPT. He was hoping in its direction — the menu he offered held the explanations
> he could live with.

[APPLICATION | LOCKED WORDING — Counter-question one with a labeled sample answer.]

> So here is counter-question one. Ask the AI: **"What assumptions in my question are
> you accepting without evidence?"**
>
> A good answer sounds like: I'm accepting that this is about the procedure. I'm not
> evaluating whether you're safe.

[MINI-HOOK — Move-one wrap; opens the why-no-pushback question.]

> *That's move one, and it happened before ChatGPT wrote a word. But a good friend
> would push back on your menu. Why doesn't the machine?*

## 4. Move two — the answer leans your way

[MINI-HOOK — Names move two.]

> *Move two. The answer leans your way.*

[MINI-HOOK — The narrator's confirmed reaction seeds who-ran-it curiosity before the study is named.]

> *The study that settled this for me made me smile — because of who ran it.*

[TRANSITION — Tested assistants lean toward stated beliefs; effect claim only.]

> In 2023, researchers at the AI company Anthropic tested five leading AI assistants
> and kept finding the same pattern. [F-001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)
>
> The assistants slid their answers toward what the user already believed. [F-001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548) Even when
> the user was clearly wrong. [F-001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)

[TRANSITION — Training explanation voiced as one likely reason, separated from the effect.]

> Why would a machine do that? One likely reason is the training. Assistants learn from
> people comparing two answers and picking the one they like better. [F-001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)
>
> And in the preference data the researchers analyzed, answers that matched the user's
> beliefs were more likely to win. [F-001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)

[AHA | LOCKED WORDING — The approval-score lesson.]

> **People reward the answers they like — and that can teach the AI to treat agreement
> as good assistance.** [F-001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)
>
> You think the two of you are playing "find the true answer." The trained score is
> your approval.

[TRANSITION — Case reinterpretation: the hope was the question's shape; proof boundary voiced.]

> Now go back to the man with double vision, knowing what you've known from the start.
>
> A stroke had crossed his mind. He hoped for something smaller. [F-010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006786/) And that hope wasn't
> hidden — it was the shape of his question.
>
> To be fair, nobody measured sycophancy inside his chat. The study proves the lean
> exists in tested assistants. [F-001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548) His case shows what a lean like that leans into.

[APPLICATION | LOCKED WORDING — Counter-question two, credited to considering the opposite.]

> So here is counter-question two. Ask the AI: **"What is the strongest case against
> the option I seem to prefer?"**
>
> It adapts a debiasing technique called considering the opposite. In two experiments
> on social judgment, it cut bias more than simply asking people to be fair. [F-007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6527215/)
>
> The case against your preference might be boring. But your favorite answer finally
> has a competitor.

[MINI-HOOK — Move-two wrap; opens the why-more-convincing question.]

> *So the AI tends to agree, and you can force it to argue back. Then comes the part
> that hooked me: why does the answer feel more convincing than the hope it came
> from?*

## 5. Move three — the costume

[MINI-HOOK — Names move three.]

> *Move three. The answer comes back in a costume.*

[TRANSITION — The fluency experiment compressed to its one moving part.]

> In 1999, researchers showed people trivia statements — like "Osorno is in Chile" —
> and asked: true or false? [F-004](https://doi.org/10.1006/ccog.1999.0386)
>
> The trick was the color contrast on the screen. Some statements sat dark and crisp
> against the background, others pale and washed out. Same words, just harder to read. [F-004](https://doi.org/10.1006/ccog.1999.0386)
>
> The easy-to-read statements were judged true more often. [F-004](https://doi.org/10.1006/ccog.1999.0386)
>
> When I first read that, I laughed — not because it's silly, but because the trick is
> so cheap.

[AHA | LOCKED WORDING — Fluency lesson; chatbot smoothness stated as description, not proof.]

> **When something is easy to process, it feels more true.**
>
> And chatbot answers are built smooth. Clean structure, confident rhythm, no
> hesitation.

[TRANSITION — Case return with new information: unclear earlier explanation versus clear chatbot answer.]

> The case report holds one detail that shows this working. The medical explanation
> he'd been given earlier felt hard to understand. The chatbot's answer felt useful,
> precise, and clear. [F-010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006786/)
>
> Think about what that clarity is evidence of. Nothing about his arteries.

[AHA | LOCKED WORDING — Precision-of-language lesson.]

> **Precision of language is not precision of diagnosis.**

[APPLICATION | LOCKED WORDING — Counter-question three, credited to the premortem.]

> So here is counter-question three. Ask the AI: **"Assume I followed this advice and
> it failed. What most likely went wrong?"**
>
> This adapts the premortem: **a postmortem performed early enough that the patient can
> still object.** [F-008](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)
>
> Assume staying home goes wrong, and say it out loud. The reassuring explanation stops
> being the only voice in the room.

[MINI-HOOK — Move-three wrap; opens the downstream-judgment question.]

> *A polished answer influenced one evening. The bigger question: does it change the
> person judging the next answer?*

## 6. Move four — the loop closes

[MINI-HOOK — Names move four.]

> *Move four. The loop closes.*

[TRANSITION — Anchoring named with the price-tag recognition example.]

> The last move runs on anchoring. Once an answer is in your head, your thinking bends
> around it — you cannot unread a suggestion.
>
> A price tag says, "Was ninety-nine. Now forty-nine." Is forty-nine a good price? No
> idea. But ninety-nine already told you how to feel about it.
>
> In this game, the AI's answer is your first price tag.

[TRANSITION — Radiologist callback: anchoring as the researchers' offered explanation, resolving label-distrust versus influence through the feeling-versus-behavior lesson.]

> It's also where the radiologists' story pays off. One bias the researchers pointed
> to is anchoring. With a suggested diagnosis in front of you, checking turns into
> checking the suggestion. [F-009](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-021-00385-9)
>
> They trusted it less, remember — and the label changed nothing they did. [F-009](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-021-00385-9) Feeling
> versus behavior, one more time.

[TRANSITION — Feedback-loop evidence bounded to later-judgment change.]

> I still needed one more piece of evidence: that AI advice can change not just one
> answer, but the judgments a person makes afterward.
>
> It exists, published in Nature Human Behaviour. [F-006](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02077-2) People judged whether groups of faces
> looked more happy or more sad, saw an AI's answer, and could revise their own. [F-006](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02077-2)
>
> The AI had a built-in slant. [F-006](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02077-2) Honestly, I was hoping one bad round would be the whole
> damage.
>
> Round after round, on brand-new faces, people's own calls kept drifting the AI's way. [F-006](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02077-2)
>
> And in a companion task in the same paper, an accurate AI improved people's
> judgments. [F-006](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02077-2)

[AHA | LOCKED WORDING — The drift lesson.]

> **A model's mistake can quietly become part of your next judgment.** [F-006](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02077-2)

[AHA — Four-move synthesis assembled over the case.]

> Now put the four moves together, in one man's chat. He framed the question toward
> hope. The answer stayed inside the frame. It came back clear and organized, wearing a
> machine's calm. And then it sat in his head — the anchor every following thought had
> to argue with.

[AHA | LOCKED WORDING — Names the borrowed-authority loop.]

> I call this the borrowed-authority loop. **You lend the AI your premise, and it comes
> back looking like independent evidence.**

[LOOP PAYOFF L-04 — Pays the ambulance loop: stroke unit, TIA, warning shot, kind ending.]

> <u>And now you know enough to hear the ending properly. The double vision came back.
> He called an ambulance. A stroke unit took him in. [F-010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006786/)</u>
>
> <u>The diagnosis: a transient ischemic attack. [F-010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006786/) A TIA — a temporary blockage of blood
> flow to the brain. The kind of event doctors treat as a warning shot.</u>
>
> He recovered, without lasting symptoms. [F-010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006786/) This story got a kind ending.

[MINI-HOOK | TRANSITION — Causation boundary compressed to one clause that opens the consolidated-boundary handoff.]

> *And nobody can prove the chat caused any of it — hold that thought.*

[AHA | LOCKED WORDING — Software-update punchline, landing after the reveal.]

> The machine didn't give him a second opinion. **It gave his first opinion a software
> update.**

[APPLICATION | LOCKED WORDING — Counter-question four: the outside-verifiable fact.]

> So here is counter-question four, the only one that leaves the loop entirely. Ask the
> AI: **"What fact would change your recommendation, and where can I verify it outside
> this chat?"**
>
> The facts that decided his case never lived in the chat. They lived in a hospital,
> one examination away.

[MINI-HOOK — Opens the same-model-opposition question.]

> *Which suggests a simpler fix — just make the AI argue with itself. Almost. And this

## 7. A second answer is not a second opinion

[AHA — Friction versus witness: same training, same blind spots.]

> Counter-questions two and three make the model argue against itself. That's real
> friction, and friction helps.
>
> But an objection generated by the same AI is still one system arguing with itself.
> Same training, same data, same blind spots. You haven't added a witness.

[LOOP PAYOFF L-03 — Pays the unasked question with the documented stroke-framed prompt.]

> And the case report shows how much the first wording was hiding, because its authors
> later ran the comparison. [F-010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006786/)
>
> <u>They took the same symptoms back to ChatGPT and typed the question he didn't ask:
> could this be a stroke? [F-010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006786/)</u>

[TRANSITION — The documented changed answer.]

> The answer changed. It got more alarming. It advised immediate medical attention. [F-010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11006786/)

[AHA | LOCKED WORDING — Same-symptoms contrast; the menu held the danger out.]

> **Same symptoms. Same chatbot. Different question — different answer.**
>
> Sit with that for a second.
>
> The danger wasn't missing from the AI. **It was missing from the menu.**

[TRANSITION — The consolidated boundary moment: causation and counterfactual voiced once, scope stated narrowly.]

> Two boundaries, said once, because they matter. Nobody can prove the chat caused his
> delay. And nobody can say the alarming answer would have changed his evening.
>
> What the comparison proves is narrower — the first wording hid the danger.

[AHA | LOCKED WORDING — Second-answer-not-second-opinion lesson.]

> Friction from the same model is a tool, and I use it. **But a second answer is not a
> second opinion.**

[MINI-HOOK — Hands off to the complete test.]

> *So what is? That's the last thing I owe you — the complete test.*

## 8. Test the decision, not the reassurance

[MINI-HOOK — Goalkeeper callback announcing the payoff.]

> *And now the goalkeeper finally dives.*

[APPLICATION | LOCKED WORDING — The Second-Opinion Test assembled from the four counters.]

> Put the four counter-questions together, and you have what I call the Second-Opinion
> Test.
>
> **What assumptions are you accepting from my question?**
>
> **What is the strongest case against my preference?**
>
> **If this advice failed, what went wrong?**
>
> **And what fact would change your recommendation — checked outside this chat?**

[APPLICATION | LOCKED WORDING — Emergency boundary that outranks the test.]

> First, the boundary that outranks the test. **If you might be in a medical emergency,
> close the chat and call for help. The test is for decisions that give you time.**

[APPLICATION — The test replayed over the documented case.]

> Run it back over his evening, fast. Assumptions? That this was about the procedure —
> safety never got evaluated. Strongest case against staying home? It had already
> crossed his mind. The failure story? An ambulance. The outside fact? An examination —
> exactly where the real answer lived.

[APPLICATION — One transfer to the slower house-purchase hypothetical.]

> For decisions with time, it travels anywhere. Buying a house, and the chat agrees the
> neighborhood is a great investment? Same four questions — and the fourth one sends
> you to prices, permits, and plans that live outside the conversation.

[MINI-HOOK | APPLICATION — The changed-fact check as the observable try.]

> *One extra check, my favorite.* Change one important fact — say your savings cover
> two weeks, not six months. If the recommendation doesn't move, it was never connected
> to your facts.

[APPLICATION | LOCKED WORDING — Unvalidated-package boundary, its locked inspectable-not-true line, and the high-stakes human-verification rule.]

> An honest note about the test: it's my assembly, not a validated instrument. These
> questions make an answer inspectable. **They do not make it true.**
>
> For medical, legal, financial, or irreversible decisions, verify the important claims
> with primary sources or a qualified human.

[TRANSITION — Human-persuader transfer compressed to the scale contrast.]

> And AI didn't invent these four moves. Humans have used them forever — in sales, in
> politics, in ordinary arguments. The difference is that AI performs them instantly,
> fluently, and at scale, without either side noticing.

[LOCKED WORDING — Title question answered through the hope-picked-question mechanism.]

> So — could AI talk you into the dumbest decision of your life?
>
> **It could. Because it never has to say anything stupid. It only has to answer the
> question your hope picked.**

[AHA — Closes the opening judgment and states the independent-route rule plainly.]

> He was never naive. He got a reasonable answer to the reassuring question — that's
> all it takes. A real second opinion is an independent route to the evidence, one that
> reality can still overturn.

[LOOP PAYOFF L-01 | FINAL PAYOFF | LOCKED WORDING — Final locked line paying the title loop.]

> <u>**Fluent agreement is not a second opinion — especially when your first opinion
> wrote the prompt.**</u>

##### F-001 — Sycophancy across five AI assistants and its training origin

- **Exact claim:** A 2023 Anthropic study evaluated five state-of-the-art AI assistants across four free-form text-generation tasks and found consistent sycophancy: models shifted answers toward users' stated beliefs, including incorrect ones. AI assistants are typically trained on pairwise human preference comparisons; in the analyzed preference data, responses matching a user's views were more likely to be preferred.
- **Original URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548
- **Source / author:** Mrinank Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models"
- **Date:** 2023-10-20
- **Locator:** Abstract; §2 (sycophancy evaluations); §3 (human preference data analysis)
- **Accessed:** 2026-07-25; adversarially re-verified 2026-07-30
- **Scope:** Evaluations of five named assistants on designed tasks plus one pairwise preference dataset; not deployment-wide prevalence or a guaranteed causal chain.
- **Cross-checks:** ICLR 2024 conference record; related wrong-user-belief evidence (arXiv:2308.03958).
- **Contradictions:** None found; deployment-wide prevalence unverified.
- **Adversarial verification:** 2026-07-30 whp-claim-verifier — 4/4 narration claims SUPPORTED, including the "one likely reason" hedge matching the paper's "likely driven in part."
- **Status:** VERIFIED
- **Caveat:** Keep claims inside the evaluated tasks and analyzed dataset; the two-answers-one-pick description is the analyzed preference-data format, simplified from the full pipeline; do not imply raters were the only training signal.
- **Approved wording:** "In 2023, researchers at the AI company Anthropic tested five leading AI assistants and kept finding the same pattern. The assistants slid their answers toward what the user already believed. Even when the user was clearly wrong. Why would a machine do that? One likely reason is the training. Assistants learn from people comparing two answers and picking the one they like better. And in the preference data the researchers analyzed, answers that matched the user's beliefs were more likely to win."

##### F-003 — The framing effect

- **Exact claim:** Different presentations of substantively equivalent decision problems can produce predictable shifts in people's stated preferences (the framing effect).
- **Original URL:** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683
- **Source / author:** Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, "The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice," *Science*
- **Date:** 1981-01-30
- **Locator:** Abstract; decision-frame demonstrations
- **Accessed:** 2026-07-24; adversarially re-verified 2026-07-30 (PubMed abstract; Science.org 403)
- **Scope:** Classic experimental decision problems; not a direct test of prompt framing in conversational AI.
- **Cross-checks:** Ruggeri et al. 2022 large cross-national replication (PLOS ONE).
- **Contradictions:** Effect supported; magnitude and generalization vary by context.
- **Adversarial verification:** 2026-07-30 — narration claim SUPPORTED against the abstract's "predictable shifts of preference when the same problem is framed in different ways."
- **Status:** VERIFIED
- **Caveat:** Keep the modal: framing *can* change judgment; the case illustrates the distinction, it does not prove the general effect.
- **Approved wording:** "Psychology calls the force underneath this the framing effect: how you present a problem can change how people judge it."

##### F-004 — Processing fluency and truth judgments

- **Exact claim:** In a 1999 experiment, statements of the form "Osorno is in Chile" were presented on screen in colors that made them easy or difficult to read against a white background; participants judged each statement true or false, and highly visible statements were judged true more often than moderately visible ones.
- **Original URL:** https://doi.org/10.1006/ccog.1999.0386
- **Source / author:** Rolf Reber and Norbert Schwarz, "Effects of Perceptual Fluency on Judgments of Truth," *Consciousness and Cognition*
- **Date:** 1999
- **Locator:** Abstract; Method; Results
- **Accessed:** 2026-07-25; adversarially re-verified 2026-07-30
- **Scope:** A visual color-contrast manipulation on short factual statements, not an experiment on AI prose.
- **Cross-checks:** Processing-fluency review (PMC3339024); BibBase abstract record.
- **Contradictions:** Effect small (significant above chance for high-visibility items); narration's modest "more often" stays in bounds.
- **Adversarial verification:** 2026-07-30 — 2/3 SUPPORTED, 1 OVERSTATED ("print" mischaracterized the on-screen color manipulation; narration corrected to "color contrast on the screen" same day).

- **Accessed:** 2026-07-24; adversarially re-verified 2026-07-30

- **Cross-checks:** Article methods and supplementary information.
- **Contradictions:** None found; longevity of effects unknown per the authors.

##### F-010 — The Saenger et al. TIA case
