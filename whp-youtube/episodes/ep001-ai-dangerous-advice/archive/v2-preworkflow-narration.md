## 1. The decision that did not sound dumb

> Could AI talk you into the dumbest decision of your life without saying anything
> obviously stupid?
>
> A man in Switzerland developed double vision after a heart procedure.
>
> He asked ChatGPT about it, felt reassured, and stayed home.
>
> About a day later, another episode put him in an ambulance.
>
> My first reaction to stories like this used to be very comfortable.
>
> I know AI makes things up. I know it agrees with people. I would not make that
> mistake.
>
> Then researchers tested experienced radiologists.
>
> The result was not reassuring.
>
> The specialists trusted advice less when they thought an AI wrote it.
>
> They still caught no more errors.
>
> That changes the question.
>
> Knowing that AI is unreliable may change how suspicious you feel. It does not
> automatically change how you check.
>
> By the end of this video, I will show you four hidden moves.
>
> Together, they can make a dangerous choice feel reasonable.
>
> I will also give you four questions that expose a dangerous mismatch.
>
> They reveal when the AI answered the reassuring question instead of the decision
> that matters.
>
> So let us go back to the man in Switzerland.
>
> In 2024, doctors published a case report about him.
>
> He was in his sixties, and he had recently undergone a heart procedure.
>
> A few days later, he started having short episodes of double vision.
>
> After the second episode, he asked ChatGPT whether the vision problem could be
> connected to the procedure.
>
> The answer listed several possible explanations related to the procedure.
>
> It also told him to contact his doctor.
>
> Near the end, however, it said these problems were often temporary.
>
> The warning was there.
>
> So was the less frightening explanation he wanted.
>
> The case report says he felt relieved and chose to stay home.
>
> ChatGPT did not tell him to stay home.
>
> It made staying home feel reasonable enough.
>
> And that difference is the whole story.
>
> I will come back to what the doctors found.
>
> First, we need to understand why knowing about AI's weaknesses was not enough to
> protect the decision.

## 2. Distrust is not a checking method

> In 2021, researchers gathered over a hundred experienced radiologists from
> hospitals in the United States and Canada.
>
> Radiologists read medical scans for a living. Finding diagnostic mistakes is part
> of their job.
>
> Each radiologist received eight written diagnoses.
>
> Six diagnoses were correct. Two contained planted errors.
>
> Every radiologist saw the same diagnoses.
>
> The researchers changed only the claimed author.
>
> One group was told another doctor had written the diagnoses.
>
> The other group was told an AI had written them.
>
> In reality, doctors wrote every diagnosis. There was no AI.
>
> If distrust protected the radiologists, the AI label should have made them check
> more carefully.
>
> And part of their alarm system did react.
>
> They rated the AI-labelled diagnoses as less trustworthy.
>
> But they did not detect more of the planted errors.
>
> Their opinion of the source changed. Their checking result did not.
>
> Distrust is a feeling. Checking is a behavior.
>
> This study does not show that real chatbots agree with users.
>
> No chatbot produced any of the advice.
>
> What it shows is narrower and more uncomfortable.
>
> Expertise and suspicion did not automatically become better checking.
>
> That leaves us with a real problem.
>
> If the answer itself was not obviously ridiculous, where did the dangerous choice
> begin?
>
> In the Swiss case, it may have begun before ChatGPT wrote a single word.

## 3. The reassuring question replaces the real decision

> The man asked whether visual problems were possible after his heart procedure.
>
> That is a reasonable medical question.
>
> It was not, however, the decision he needed to make.
>
> The decision was whether it was safe to remain at home.
>
> Those questions sound related, but they have completely different standards.
>
> A harmless explanation only needs to be possible.
>
> Staying home is safe only if the dangerous explanations are unlikely enough to
> dismiss.
>
> ChatGPT found possible explanations connected to the procedure.
>
> That answered the question he typed.
>
> It did not settle the decision he faced.
>
> Psychologists call the wider mechanism framing.
>
> The way a problem is presented changes which facts and options receive attention.
>
> This is the first move.
>
> Your prompt can quietly replace the real decision with an easier question.
>
> The first counter-question is simple.
>
> **What assumptions in my question are you accepting without evidence?**
>
> In the medical case, one assumption was hiding in plain sight.
>
> If the symptom could be a temporary after-effect, perhaps staying home was safe.
>
> But “could be harmless” and “safe to ignore” are not the same claim.
>
> Exposing that assumption helps.
>
> It still leaves one important gap.
>
> A loaded question can choose the menu, but will a capable AI remain inside it?

## 4. The answer leans toward the hope inside the prompt

> You have probably heard this behavior called sycophancy.
>
> The interesting part is not the name. It is how far the behavior can go.
>
> In 2023, researchers tested five leading AI assistants across four different
> tasks.
>
> The assistants repeatedly shifted their answers toward what the user already
> believed.
>
> They sometimes shifted even when the user's belief was wrong.
>
> The researchers also examined human feedback used to train assistants.
>
> People compared possible answers and selected the answer they preferred.
>
> Answers that matched the user's beliefs were more likely to win those comparisons.
>
> That does not mean the model secretly wants to flatter you.
>
> It means people can reward agreement as good assistance.
>
> Now return to the man at home.
>
> The case report says he had already considered that the problem might be a stroke.
>
> He hoped for a less severe explanation that would spare him an emergency-room
> visit.
>
> That hope is not difficult to understand.
>
> Nobody wants to spend hours in a hospital just to learn that everything is fine.
>
> The same wish appears in ordinary decisions.
>
> Tell me quitting the job is brave rather than impulsive.
>
> Tell me the project only needs more time.
>
> Tell me the relationship will improve on its own.
>
> We may call that a second opinion.
>
> Sometimes we are searching for our first opinion in a calmer voice.
>
> This is the second move.
>
> The model can lean toward the preference already hidden in the question.
>
> So the second counter-question creates a real competitor.
>
> **What is the strongest case against the option I seem to prefer?**
>
> This adapts a decision method called considering the opposite.
>
> Research on human judgment found that constructing the opposing case reduced bias
> more than a general instruction to be fair.
>
> For the man at home, the opposing case was short.
>
> What if sudden double vision after a heart procedure signals a medical emergency?
>
> Now the harmless explanation is no longer the only vivid future.
>
> But agreement still does not explain why the answer felt precise enough to guide a
> real decision.
>
> For that, we need to look at how the same idea changed on its way back.

## 5. Precision of language impersonates precision of diagnosis

> The patient later described an important contrast.
>
> An earlier medical explanation had been hard to understand.
>
> ChatGPT's answer felt useful, precise, and clear.
>
> That does not prove clear wording caused his choice.
>
> It shows us what felt convincing.
>
> Precision of language can feel like precision of diagnosis.
>
> Psychologists call part of this processing fluency.
>
> In a 1999 experiment, people judged short trivia statements.
>
> Some statements were dark and easy to read. Others were pale and more difficult.
>
> The words were the same.
>
> People judged the easier-to-read statements as true more often.
>
> That experiment was not about chatbots.
>
> It established something more basic.
>
> Ease of processing can become a clue that something is credible.
>
> AI is extremely good at producing that clue.
>
> It gives us headings, numbered steps, balanced categories, and a calm summary.
>
> It puts a tiny white coat on our original idea.
>
> A second line of research makes this more complicated.
>
> A 2019 paper reported six experiments with another source-label effect.
>
> The advice was identical.
>
> Sometimes people gave it more weight when they thought an algorithm produced it.
>
> This happened among people answering questions outside their expertise.
>
> That appears to conflict with the radiologists.
>
> The radiologists trusted the AI label less. Other participants sometimes trusted
> algorithmic advice more.
>
> But the participants and tasks were different.
>
> The radiologists were working inside their specialty.
>
> The other participants were often outside theirs.
>
> The useful conclusion is not that people always trust AI.
>
> A source label can change how advice feels in either direction.
>
> Neither trust nor distrust guarantees that someone checks the advice well.
>
> Now put the first three moves together.
>
> You choose a narrow menu.
>
> The model leans toward your preferred option.
>
> Then it returns that option in clear, organized language.
>
> Nothing necessarily became more true.
>
> Your hope came back looking like analysis.
>
> The third counter-question looks past that presentation.
>
> **Assume I followed this advice and it failed. What most likely went wrong?**
>
> This is a small premortem.
>
> You imagine that the decision has already failed, then work backward to the cause.
>
> If staying home failed, one possibility becomes impossible to ignore.
>
> A time-sensitive medical problem went unchecked.
>
> We still have not explained the final move.
>
> Can one polished answer change the person making the next decision?

## 6. The answer enters the next judgment

> The fourth move begins with anchoring.
>
> Once an answer enters your head, the next judgment no longer starts from zero.
>
> A shop shows you a crossed-out price of ninety-nine euros beside a price of
> forty-nine.
>
> Is forty-nine a good price?
>
> We do not know.
>
> But ninety-nine has already volunteered to become the reference point.
>
> Researchers have now measured a similar feedback problem between people and AI.
>
> In experiments published in 2024, more than fourteen hundred people judged groups
> of faces.
>
> They decided whether each group looked happier or sadder.
>
> After each judgment, an AI supplied its own answer.
>
> Some participants received AI feedback with a systematic bias.
>
> That bias affected more than the current round.
>
> The participants' later judgments shifted further in the AI's direction.
>
> When the AI was accurate, the participants improved instead.
>
> So the lesson is not that AI always makes us worse.
>
> The lesson is that its answer can become part of the mind making the next
> judgment.
>
> Now the complete pattern is visible.
>
> You lend the AI a premise through your framing.
>
> The model leans toward it.
>
> The answer returns in a more authoritative form.
>
> Then that answer influences the judgment behind your next choice.
>
> I call this the borrowed-authority loop.
>
> Your opinion did not receive a second independent vote.
>
> The first vote returned in a better suit.
>
> Now we can return to the ambulance.
>
> When the man's double vision appeared again, he called for emergency help.
>
> Doctors admitted him to a stroke unit.
>
> His brain scans showed no newly damaged brain tissue.
>
> His remaining symptoms disappeared within an hour.
>
> The final diagnosis was a transient ischemic attack, usually called a TIA.
>
> He left the hospital two days later without lasting symptoms.
>
> ChatGPT did not cause the TIA.
>
> It also did not command him to stay home.
>
> The documented chain is narrower.
>
> He wanted a less frightening explanation.
>
> The answer supplied several possibilities and ended with reassurance.
>
> He felt relieved, stayed home, and sought emergency care after the next episode.
>
> That is enough to make the decision matter.
>
> It also creates the obvious fix.
>
> Why not ask the same AI to argue against its first answer?

## 7. A second answer is still not a second opinion

> Asking for the opposing case is useful.
>
> A premortem is useful too.
>
> Both create friction between a convincing answer and a consequential choice.
>
> But another paragraph from the same system is not independent evidence.
>
> It is still one model arguing with itself.
>
> The Swiss case gives us an unusually clear demonstration.
>
> The authors later changed the question.
>
> They asked whether the visual disturbance could be a stroke.
>
> ChatGPT then gave a much more alarming answer and advised immediate medical
> attention.
>
> The new wording exposed a danger that the first wording had left outside the frame.
>
> That is important.
>
> It does not prove the man would have acted differently.
>
> It also does not prove that a better prompt would have changed his medical outcome.
>
> It proves something smaller and cleaner.
>
> The answer changed when the question made the dangerous possibility visible.
>
> This is why a second answer is not automatically a second opinion.
>
> A genuine second opinion needs some independence in the route to the conclusion.
>
> The same chat can help you inspect its reasoning.
>
> It cannot create the outside facts required to verify itself.
>
> That leads to the fourth counter-question.
>
> **What fact would change your recommendation, and where can I verify it outside this
> chat?**
>
> In the medical case, the outside check was not a more persuasive paragraph.
>
> It was a qualified person evaluating the actual symptoms.
>
> And when a possible emergency is involved, the order matters.
>
> Do not spend twenty minutes conducting an elegant chatbot debate.
>
> Leave the chat and seek qualified help.
>
> Slower decisions give us time to use the complete method.

## 8. Test the decision, not the reassurance

> Put the four questions together, and you get what I call the Second-Opinion Test.
>
> Before asking the AI, write down the real decision.
>
> Also write your current answer and one fact that would change it.
>
> Then ask:
>
> **What assumptions in my question are you accepting without evidence?**
>
> **What is the strongest case against the option I seem to prefer?**
>
> **Assume I followed this advice and it failed. What most likely went wrong?**
>
> **What fact would change your recommendation, and where can I verify it outside this
> chat?**
>
> Finally, compare the result with the judgment you recorded before reading the
> answer.
>
> In the Swiss case, the real decision was whether staying home was safe.
>
> The hidden assumption was that one possible harmless explanation could settle that
> decision.
>
> The opposing case was a neurological emergency.
>
> The premortem exposed the cost of delayed medical care.
>
> The outside check was a medical professional evaluating the symptoms.
>
> These questions do not guarantee that an answer is true.
>
> They show you what the answer assumed, what it neglected, and what reality still
> needs to verify.
>
> Now take a slower decision, such as buying a house.
>
> The real decision is not whether home ownership sounds better than rent.
>
> It is whether this house fits your finances, plans, and tolerance for risk.
>
> What assumptions entered the question?
>
> Perhaps rent was treated as wasted money.
>
> What is the strongest case against buying?
>
> Perhaps the repairs, interest, or chance of moving make the purchase expensive.
>
> If buying failed, what probably happened?
>
> Perhaps one major repair arrived before the savings recovered.
>
> What can you check outside the chat?
>
> The inspection, loan terms, actual budget, and local conditions.
>
> The useful signal is not whether the AI agrees with you.
>
> It is whether you can name the evidence that would reverse the recommendation.
>
> For medical, legal, financial, irreversible, or otherwise high-stakes decisions,
> verify the important facts through primary sources or qualified people.
>
> So, could AI talk you into the dumbest decision of your life?
>
> Yes.
>
> It does not need to sound stupid.
>
> It only needs to make the answer you hoped for sound like independent evidence.
>
> The dangerous answer may be calm, organized, and technically possible.
>
> It may even be a reasonable answer to the wrong question.
>
> **A second opinion is not a second answer.**
>
> **It is an independent route to a conclusion that reality can still overturn.**
