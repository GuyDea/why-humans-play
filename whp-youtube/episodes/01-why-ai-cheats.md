## Beat 03 — Goal, proxy, optimizer, gap
_Time: 01:35–02:45 · Target: ~180 words_

### Narration
> That gives us four parts. First, the broader goal: the result the designer cares about. Second, a measurable proxy: the piece the system can score. Third, an optimizer choosing among available strategies according to that score. And fourth, an ignored dimension: something relevant that the scoreboard cannot see.
>
> Put them together and a hidden game appears. The designer chooses the scoreboard. The score ranks strategies. The optimizer searches among them. What the score cannot see sits outside the rewarded boundary.
>
> Skalse and colleagues formalize a narrow version in a bounded reinforcement-learning model. A proxy score can rank one strategy higher even when the underlying goal—what the paper calls the true reward—ranks it lower. In plain language: the scoreboard can prefer the move the goal rejects.
>
> Researchers call that possibility reward hacking. DeepMind uses “specification gaming” for behavior that satisfies the literal objective without achieving the intended outcome. A hackable proxy does not guarantee an optimizer will find that strategy, and this model does not say every optimizer or metric fails. It points to the gap between earning points and winning.

## Beat 07 — Inspect an everyday score

>

#### F-002 — Bounded reward-hacking mechanism and terminology

- **Exact claim:** In the bounded reinforcement-learning formalism of Skalse et al., an imperfect proxy is hackable when it ranks one policy above another that the true reward ranks below; DeepMind uses “specification gaming” for behavior that satisfies a literal specification without achieving the intended outcome.

- **Date:** 2022 (NeurIPS 2022; arXiv v2 dated 2025-03-05).
- **Locator:** Abstract and Section 1, paper pages 1–2; Section 4.2, Definition 1, paper page 5; Section 6.1 “Limitations,” paper page 9.
- **Accessed:** 2026-07-21.
- **Scope:** The formal claim concerns proxy and true rewards over policies in the paper's finite-MDP, Markov-reward framework. DeepMind supplies only the additional “specification gaming” terminology; it does not cross-check the Skalse formalism or erase that formal scope.
- **Cross-checks:** No independent cross-check of the Skalse formalism is claimed. Additional terminology source: [DeepMind, “Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity”](https://deepmind.google/blog/specification-gaming-the-flip-side-of-ai-ingenuity/), opening definition and reward-specification discussion; it supports only the literal-objective/intended-outcome terminology from a separate author chain, not the paper's formal policy-ordering result.
- **Contradictions:** Skalse et al. paper — COMPLETE — [abstract, Sections 1–6, appendices, definitions, and limitations checked; directly supports the policy-ordering definition while limiting it to finite MDPs with Markov rewards and warning that hackability does not guarantee observed hacking; retain `VERIFIED` with the formal boundary]; DeepMind specification-gaming review — COMPLETE — [full article checked; supports only the literal-specification versus intended-outcome terminology and presents examples, but neither tests nor corroborates the Skalse formal definition; use it as an additional terminology source and keep each subclaim's provenance explicit].
- **Status:** VERIFIED
- **Caveat:** A hackable proxy does not guarantee that optimization will produce reward hacking, and the definition does not show that every optimizer or metric fails. “Cheat” is the episode's shorthand for a designer's judgment about an outcome, not a technical claim about a system's intent, consciousness, or moral character.
- **Approved wording:** “In this bounded reinforcement-learning model, a proxy score can rank one strategy higher even when the underlying goal—what the paper formalizes as the true reward—ranks it lower.
