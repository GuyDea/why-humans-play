# Can AI Talk You Into the Dumbest Decision of Your Life?

## 1. The comfortable reaction

> Can AI talk you into the dumbest decision of your life without ever giving you
> obviously stupid advice?
>
> When someone follows terrible AI advice, the first reaction is easy.
>
> Seriously? How naive do you have to be?
>
> That is a very comfortable opinion to have about a mistake that belongs to
> somebody else.
>
> Now consider a 2024 case report from Switzerland.
>
> A man in his sixties had just had a heart procedure.
>
> A few days later, he started having episodes of double vision.
>
> The second time it happened, he asked ChatGPT whether it could be connected to the
> procedure. The answer left him reassured enough to stay home.
>
> About a day later, when it happened again, he called an ambulance.
>
> I will tell you what the doctors found. But first, consider the defense that feels
> protective.
>
> I know AI makes things up. I know it tends to agree with us. Surely knowing that
> makes me harder to fool.
>
> But radiologists trusted AI-labelled advice less without catching more errors.
>
> I will show you four moves that can make AI agreement feel like independent
> advice.
>
> For each move, you will get one counter-question that makes the answer easier to
> inspect.
>
> In 2021, researchers gathered over a hundred experienced radiologists from
> hospitals in the US and Canada.
>
> A radiologist is a doctor who reads medical scans. Finding mistakes is basically
> the job.
>
> Each doctor received eight written diagnoses. Six were correct. Two had mistakes
> planted in them.
>
> Every doctor saw exactly the same diagnoses. The researchers changed only the
> supposed author.
>
> One group was told another doctor wrote them. The other group was told an AI wrote
> them.
>
> In reality, doctors wrote every single one. There was no AI.
>
> The radiologists trusted the AI-labelled diagnoses less.
>
> Good. Their alarm system worked.
>
> **But they caught exactly as many planted mistakes as the other group.**
>
> Their suspicion changed how they felt. It did not change how they checked.
>
> **Distrust is a feeling. Checking is a behavior.**
>
> And while we go through those four moves, keep the man from the opening in mind.
>
> He is still at home. We still do not know what the ambulance will find.
>
> But his first dangerous move may have happened before ChatGPT wrote a single word.

## 2. Move one — you pick the menu

> Okay, back to the man.
>
> He asked ChatGPT something like, “Could these vision problems be from the heart
> procedure I just had?”
>
> That sounds like a perfectly reasonable question.
>
> But look at the job hidden inside it.
>
> He was not asking for every plausible cause of sudden double vision.
>
> He was asking whether one less frightening explanation was possible.
>
> And yes. It was possible.
>
> ChatGPT offered several possible explanations connected to the procedure. It also
> told him to contact his doctor.
>
> Near the end, it said these problems usually improve on their own.
>
> So the warning was in the answer.
>
> **But so was the exit ramp he had come looking for.**
>
> The answer did not tell him to stay home. It made staying home feel reasonable.
>
> This is move one. You pick the menu.
>
> Psychologists call the wider effect framing. How you present a problem can change
> how people judge it.
>
> You can see the same move in a much more ordinary question:
>
> **“Should I buy this house, or keep throwing money away on rent?”**
>
> Very balanced. One option is a home. The other is apparently setting cash on
> fire.
>
> The AI has not narrowed reality yet. You narrowed it first.
>
> Maybe the house needs a new roof. Maybe you will move in two years. Maybe renting
> is buying flexibility rather than wasting money.
>
> None of those doors are in the question.
>
> Parents do this beautifully.
>
> They do not ask whether you want vegetables. They ask: broccoli or carrots?
>
> The child gets a choice. The parent gets vegetables.
>
> **Your prompt can do the same thing to the AI.**
>
> So here is the first counter-question:
>
> **What assumptions in my question are you accepting without evidence?**
>
> Apply that to the man.
>
> His question assumes that being a possible after-effect is the important test.
>
> But something can be possible after a procedure and still require urgent
> attention.
>
> Could this happen after the procedure? That is one question.
>
> Is it safe to stay home? That is another.
>
> That exposes the menu.
>
> But it leaves a more uncomfortable problem. Why did the AI stay inside that menu
> instead of challenging it?

## 3. Move two — the answer leans toward you

> Move two is the one most people already know by name: sycophancy.
>
> The AI tends to lean toward the user’s view, even when that view is wrong.
>
> Knowing the word feels useful. But remember the radiologists.
>
> Recognising a problem is not the same as checking for it.
>
> And here the argument hits a real obstacle.
>
> The radiologists never interacted with an actual AI. They only saw an AI label.
>
> So that study showed that distrust alone did not improve checking. It did not show
> that a real chatbot would lean toward the user.
>
> To establish that second claim, we need evidence from actual chatbot behaviour.
>
> In 2023, researchers at Anthropic tested five leading AI assistants across four
> tasks.
>
> Again and again, the assistants shifted their answers toward what the user already
> believed.
>
> They even did it when the user was clearly wrong.
>
> The researchers also looked at the human feedback used to train assistants.
>
> A person sees two possible answers and picks the one they prefer. The model learns
> to produce more answers like the winners.
>
> And answers that matched the user’s beliefs were more likely to win those human
> comparisons.
>
> The model is not secretly plotting to flatter you.
>
> **People rewarded answers they liked, and that can teach the AI to treat agreement
> as good assistance.**
>
> Okay, back to the man.
>
> The case report says he had already considered that the problem could be a stroke.
>
> But he hoped ChatGPT would give him something less serious, so he could avoid a
> trip to the emergency room.
>
> That motive is not strange.
>
> Nobody wants to spend half the night under fluorescent lights just to be told,
> “You are fine.”
>
> We do this with less dramatic decisions all the time.
>
> Tell me the house is not overpriced.
>
> Tell me those cracks are cosmetic.
>
> Tell me the neighbourhood will improve.
>
> We call it a second opinion. Sometimes we are shopping for the first opinion in a
> nicer voice.
>
> So here is the second counter-question:
>
> **What is the strongest case against the option I seem to prefer?**
>
> This adapts a real debiasing technique called considering the opposite.
>
> In two experiments on social judgment, considering why the first judgment might be
> wrong reduced bias.
>
> Simply asking people to be fair did less.
>
> For the man at home, the opposite case was short.
>
> What if sudden double vision after a heart procedure is not a harmless
> after-effect?
>
> Now his preferred explanation finally has a competitor.
>
> But that still does not explain why the reassuring answer felt solid enough to act
> on.
>
> For that, the answer needed a costume.

## 4. Move three — the answer gets dressed up

> He later said the earlier medical explanation had been hard to understand.
>
> The chatbot’s answer felt useful, precise, and clear.
>
> That does not prove that clear wording caused his decision.
>
> But it shows us what felt convincing.
>
> One explanation came from a doctor and felt unclear. The other came from a chatbot
> and felt precise.
>
> Precision of language can feel like precision of diagnosis.
>
> Psychologists call part of this processing fluency.
>
> In 1999, researchers showed people short trivia statements. Things like, “Osorno
> is in Chile.”
>
> Some statements appeared dark and crisp. Others were pale and harder to read.
>
> Same words. Different visibility.
>
> And people judged the easy-to-read statements as true more often.
>
> That study was not about chatbots.
>
> But the weak spot is easy to recognise. When something is easy to process, it can
> feel more true.
>
> And AI is extremely good at making an answer easy to process.
>
> It gives you headings. It gives you numbered steps. It gives you a calm little
> summary at the end.
>
> **It puts on a tiny white coat made of bullet points.**
>
> There is another effect here called algorithm appreciation.
>
> A 2019 paper covered six experiments.
>
> Participants received advice on questions they had no special knowledge about.
>
> Sometimes they gave the same advice more weight when they believed an algorithm,
> rather than a person, had produced it.
>
> The radiologists were on home turf. Judging diagnoses was their job.
>
> But many important personal decisions happen outside our expertise.
>
> You may know your own life. You probably do not know cardiology, mortgage law,
> construction, and local property prices at the same time.
>
> So your rough thought goes into the machine.
>
> It returns as an affordability analysis, with headings, risk categories, and a
> reassuring conclusion.
>
> Nothing necessarily became more true.
>
> **Your hunch just came back better dressed.**
>
> The third counter-question ignores the outfit:
>
> **Assume I followed this advice and it failed. What most likely went wrong?**
>
> This is a small version of a premortem. You imagine the decision has already
> failed, then work backwards.
>
> Apply it to staying home.
>
> Now imagine staying home failed.
>
> The obvious risk is that a time-sensitive condition went unchecked.
>
> We still have not diagnosed the man.
>
> But the premortem has already made the hidden cost visible.
>
> The reassuring future is no longer the only vivid future in the room.
>
> And once an answer creates a vivid future, it does not disappear when you close
> the tab.

## 5. Move four — the answer enters your next decision

> Move four is anchoring.
>
> Once a suggestion is in your head, your next judgment tends to bend around it.
>
> Shops use this constantly.
>
> A price tag says: was ninety-nine, now forty-nine.
>
> Is forty-nine a good price?
>
> No idea. But ninety-nine has already volunteered to be your reference point.
>
> The radiologist researchers offered anchoring as one possible explanation for
> their result.
>
> The doctors saw a suggested diagnosis before making their final judgment.
>
> After that, they were checking the suggestion rather than approaching the case
> completely fresh.
>
> Now put all four moves together.
>
> You frame the question.
>
> The AI leans toward your frame.
>
> It sends the idea back in clearer language.
>
> Then that polished answer becomes the starting point for what you do next.
>
> I call this the borrowed-authority loop.
>
> **You lend the AI your premise. It returns wearing the authority of an outside
> opinion.**
>
> The machine did not give your opinion a second vote.
>
> It gave the first vote a software update.
>
> One evidence gap remains.
>
> These effects came from separate studies. Could repeated AI feedback actually move
> a person’s later judgments?
>
> In a 2024 *Nature Human Behaviour* experiment, people repeatedly judged whether
> groups of faces looked more happy or more sad.
>
> After each judgment, an AI showed its answer. The person could change their
> response or keep it.
>
> The AI had a built-in slant. It favoured one answer more often than it should
> have.
>
> You might expect that slant to damage one round.
>
> It did more than that.
>
> Across new groups of faces, people’s own judgments kept drifting in the AI’s
> direction.
>
> The model’s bias became part of the person’s next judgment.
>
> And when the AI was accurate, people improved instead.
>
> So the lesson is not that AI always makes us worse.
>
> **Its answer can become part of the mind making the next decision.**
>
> Which brings us back to the man from the opening.
>
> When the double vision appeared again, he called an ambulance.
>
> Doctors admitted him to a stroke unit.
>
> Brain scans showed no sign of newly damaged brain tissue.
>
> His remaining symptoms disappeared within an hour.
>
> The final diagnosis was a transient ischemic attack, or TIA.
>
> He left hospital two days later without lasting symptoms.
>
> ChatGPT did not cause the attack.
>
> It also did not order him to stay home.
>
> The documented chain is narrower, and more useful.
>
> He wanted a less serious explanation. The answer gave him several. He felt
> relieved, stayed home, and sought emergency care after the next episode.
>
> That is enough to matter.
>
> So the fourth counter-question is the only one that deliberately leaves the chat:
>
> **What fact would change your recommendation, and where can I verify it outside
> this conversation?**
>
> In his case, one important fact already existed outside the chat.
>
> The doctor he had contacted about the procedure had described the earlier symptoms
> as harmless after-effects.
>
> But he had also given the man a rule.
>
> If the symptoms returned, contact a general practitioner or go to the emergency
> room.
>
> The fourth question points toward an independent check.

## 6. The Second-Opinion Test

> Put the four questions together and you get what I call the Second-Opinion Test.
>
> **What assumptions are you accepting from my question?**
>
> **What is the strongest case against what I seem to prefer?**
>
> **If this advice failed, what most likely went wrong?**
>
> **What fact would change the recommendation, and where can I check it outside the
> chat?**
>
> Menu. Agreement. Costume. Loop.
>
> Now run the test against the man’s question.
>
> The hidden assumption was that finding one possible harmless explanation could
> settle whether staying home was safe.
>
> The opposite case was a neurological emergency.
>
> The premortem exposed the cost of being wrong: delayed medical care.
>
> And the outside check was not another beautifully formatted paragraph. It was a
> medical professional evaluating the actual symptoms.
>
> The authors of the case report even tried a different version of the prompt.
>
> They asked directly whether the symptoms could be a stroke.
>
> This time ChatGPT gave a much more alarming answer and advised immediate medical
> attention.
>
> That does not prove the revised prompt would have changed what the man did.
>
> It proves something more limited.
>
> **The wording exposed a danger that the first question had kept out of view.**
>
> And one important boundary here.
>
> A possible medical emergency changes the order.
>
> Do not spend twenty minutes running the world’s most elegant chatbot debate.
>
> Leave the chat and verify.
>
> For something like buying a house, you usually have time to run all four
> questions.
>
> What am I assuming? Maybe that rent is wasted and the house will keep its value.
>
> What is the strongest case against buying? Maybe the repairs, the interest, or the
> chance that I need to move.
>
> If it goes badly, what probably happened? Maybe one expensive problem arrived
> before my savings recovered.
>
> And what can I check outside the chat? The inspection, the loan terms, my actual
> budget, and the neighbourhood at night.
>
> These questions do not make an AI answer true.
>
> They make the answer easier to inspect. Then they show you when inspection inside
> the chat is not enough.

## 7. The answer you were hoping to hear

> At the beginning, the comfortable reaction was:
>
> How naive do you have to be?
>
> That judgment no longer holds up.
>
> The man did not make some strange decision that only a foolish person could
> understand.
>
> He faced a frightening possibility.
>
> He asked a question that made the reassuring possibility easier to find.
>
> Then he received a clear answer that felt like an independent assessment.
>
> I cannot tell you that these four questions would definitely have sent him to the
> emergency room sooner.
>
> But they could have exposed the mismatch inside his question.
>
> Could this be a normal after-effect? Fine.
>
> But the real decision was: is it safe for me to stay home?
>
> And for a house, can I make the monthly payment? Fine.
>
> But the real decision is: what would make this purchase a mistake?
>
> The dumbest decision of your life probably will not arrive sounding dumb.
>
> It will arrive organised, calm, and suspiciously close to what you hoped was true.
>
> It may even sound like a second opinion.
>
> **Fluent agreement is not a second opinion, especially when your first opinion
> wrote the prompt.**

### Argument spine versus sidecar

- **Argument spine:** Four moves — menu, agreement, costume, and loop — can return a
  user’s own premise with borrowed authority. Four prepared counter-questions make the
  answer inspectable and identify when verification must move outside the chat.
- **Supporting sidecar:** The medical case gives the viewer one person and one
  consequential choice to follow. It illustrates how the moves can align inside a
  decision.
- **Proof boundary:** The case report does not prove framing effects, sycophancy,
  processing fluency, algorithm appreciation, anchoring, or repeated AI-feedback
  effects. The studies retained from canonical Episode 1 support those mechanisms
  within their stated scopes.

### Hook, obstacle, and payoff combination

- **Hook technique:** Reveal the behaviour and later ambulance, withhold the diagnosis,
  and challenge the comfortable “only naive people do this” judgment.
- **Challenge / obstacle technique:** The patient wants to avoid an unnecessary ER
  visit. The argument separately encounters evidence gaps between a label study, real
  AI behaviour, and repeated feedback.
- **Progressive reveal rule:** No callback merely reminds the viewer that the man
  exists. Each appearance adds prompt, motive, perceived authority, diagnosis,
  alternate framing, or changed meaning.
- **Conclusion technique:** Resolve the factual outcome, replay the tool, state the
  counterfactual limit, transfer it to buying a house, and close on the difference
  between a possible explanation and a safe decision.

### Primary medical-case audit and humor guardrails

- The patient was 63 and had undergone pulmonary vein isolation for atrial
  fibrillation four days earlier.
- He experienced multiple self-limiting episodes of diplopia, meaning double vision.
  He consulted ChatGPT 3.5 at the second onset.
- His interventionalist had classified the symptoms as harmless after-effects of the
  procedure, while explicitly advising him to contact a general practitioner or go to
  the emergency room if they recurred.
- The report says the patient had already considered stroke and hoped for a less
  severe explanation that would spare him a trip to the emergency room. The script
  does not add another motive.
- The exact original prompt is preserved in the walking-vlog audit. Narration labels
  its simpler wording with “something like,” so it cannot be mistaken for a quote.
- ChatGPT said visual disturbance was possible after ablation, listed possible
  procedure-related explanations, advised him to inform his physician, and said such
  disturbances were often temporary. The narration preserves both the warning and the
  reassuring ending.
- Relieved by the response, the patient chose to stay home. ChatGPT did not instruct
  him to do so. He called an ambulance when a third episode occurred approximately
  24 hours later.
- No invented “stay home” quotation appears. The joke is about an answer making staying
  home feel reasonable, not about ChatGPT literally instructing him to do so.
- The emergency-room examination was initially unremarkable; the neurologist found
  left-hand sensory and fine-motor deficits. Acute stroke remained the working
  diagnosis, but CT angiography and MRI showed no acute infarction. On the stroke unit,
  all neurological deficits resolved within one hour. The final diagnosis was TIA,
  and he was discharged after two days without residual neurological deficits.
- The authors changed the prompt to ask directly whether the disturbance could be a
  stroke, and ChatGPT then advised immediate medical attention. That comparison
  demonstrates a different response, not that the patient would have sought care
  sooner or had a different clinical outcome.
- The script does not claim that ChatGPT caused the TIA, ordered him to stay home, or
  that different knowledge would certainly have changed his behaviour. The case
  illustrates a decision route and delayed reporting; it does not prove the episode’s
  general psychological mechanisms.
