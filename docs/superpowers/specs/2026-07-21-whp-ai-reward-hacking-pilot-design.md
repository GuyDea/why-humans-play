## Decision

**Decision status:** Winner selected

**Confidence:** medium  
**Why it wins now:** It combines a current AI question, repeated public demand for game-playing agents that discover exploits, a defensible evidence base, a clear solo-production concept, and a distinctly WHP payoff about the hidden game created by every proxy score.  
**Strongest honest title/thumbnail promise:** *Why AI Cheats—Even When It Follows Every Rule* + an obviously wrong outcome beside a green `100%` score and the words **100% WRONG**  
**Mode:** cold-start

The selected launch approach is breadth-first:

1. AI reward hacking and human incentive design;
2. job interviews as signaling games;
3. chess and cognitive transfer; and
4. an honest evidence audit of brain games.

This sequence is an editorial direction, not a forecast or guarantee.

### Narrative architecture

1. **Opening paradox:** show a visibly wrong solution receiving a perfect score. Ask why
   a system that followed the rule still failed the task.
2. **Concrete game story:** reconstruct one primary-source exploit, then reveal the
   literal reward that made the behavior rational.
3. **Mechanism:** distinguish intended goal, measurable proxy, optimizer, and unmeasured
   dimensions; name specification gaming/reward hacking only after the viewer sees it.
4. **Capability turn:** a second game example shows that optimization can discover
   strategies the designer did not know the environment allowed.
5. **Rigor turn:** clarify that “cheat” is shorthand; environment-specific exploitation
   does not prove intent, consciousness, generalized deception, or inevitable danger.
6. **Human turn:** show two bounded analogues—one institutional metric and one everyday
   self-score—supported by incentive research, without claiming identical cognition.
7. **Viewer application, fully voiced:** insight → choose one low-risk scoreboard to
   inspect → observe the behavior it pulls toward and what disappears → boundary that
   metrics are not inherently bad and diagnosis is not causal proof → larger benefit of
   designing or choosing games more deliberately.
8. **Final payoff:** return to the perfect wrong score; reveal the designer’s proxy as
   the hidden rule and end on WHP’s choice-oriented lens.

### Three eligible opening candidates to develop before drafting

1. **Perfect score, wrong outcome — recommended:** open on a recreated agent visibly
   defeating the intended task while `100%` flashes green; land the title paradox in the
   first sentence.
2. **Hide-and-seek arms race:** begin with a simple children’s game escalating into box
   surfing and physics exploits; stronger spectacle, slower path to the human payoff.
3. **Human metric first:** open with an absurd workplace/school metric and reveal that AI
   does the same literal optimization; highly recognizable, but risks making AI feel like
   decorative click bait.

Opening 1 is recommended because it matches title, thumbnail, central question, and final
payoff without requiring copyrighted source footage.

### Failure handling and validation design

- If a load-bearing exploit cannot be verified from the primary source, replace it or cut
  it; do not retain the anecdote from secondary retellings.
- If the human analogue lacks direct support, label it as an analogy and make it
  non-load-bearing, or omit it.
- If a visual cannot be licensed or documented, use an ownable recreation that explains
  the same mechanism without copying expressive assets.
- The script must pass separate story, evidence, fact, rights, visual, animation,
  application-boundary, accessibility, personal-authenticity, and format audits plus the
  deterministic repository validator.
- Automated validation can establish structure only; it cannot declare factual,
  editorial, rights, production, or record readiness.
